The sharp increase in GB day-ahead electricity prices in 2021 and 2022 was mainly a gas-price-driven wholesale shock, amplified by the marginal pricing structure of the power market. In 2021, prices rose as economies reopened after COVID-19, increasing global fuel demand, while gas supply remained constrained. This was intensified by lower renewable/nuclear availability and specific system-tightness events, including low wind output and the IFA interconnector fire, which pushed GB day-ahead prices near record levels. 
In 2022, the Russia-Ukraine war caused a major gas-price spike and risk premium across Europe. This fed into GB electricity prices through gas-fired marginal generation. Additional pressure came from high carbon prices and French nuclear outages, which increased cross-border stress and contributed to GB becoming a net electricity exporter in 2022. 
So, the rise was not primarily caused by higher electricity demand, but by expensive gas, carbon costs, lower flexible supply margins, interconnector constraints, and European energy-security risk.

1. Executive summary
A Great Britain electricity supplier backed by renewables is not simply long power and short customer demand. It is long a weather-shaped, low-capture generation profile and short a differently-shaped demand profile, with the residual settled half-hourly against a volatile day-ahead and imbalance (cash-out) price. Three exposures dominate: volume (how many MWh actually flow, on both the generation and demand sides), price (the level paid or received, including the capture discount renewables suffer versus baseload), and volatility (the dispersion of those prices, which widens structurally as renewable penetration grows).
The central difficulty is that for a renewable supplier these three are correlated against the book. When the wind blows hard the supplier is long a large volume of cheap power; when it drops the supplier is short and must buy into a spiking market. A naive baseload hedge makes this worse. The job is therefore not to hedge price, but to hedge price conditional on volume, which is why options and structured flexibility, rather than plain forwards, are the right instruments.
This report sets out the toolkit across two families: financial options (caps, floors, collars, swing options, swaptions) and structured-flexibility structures (PPA shape choice, sleeving and peer-to-peer matching, proxy revenue swaps, capture-price hedges, battery storage with revenue stacking, tolling and day-ahead swaps, and demand-side flexibility). For each section it specifies a concrete simulation and coding project, with the quantitative metrics and visual outputs that project should produce, scaled for an engineer who can build the full stack.
Key takeaways
Forwards and PPAs handle the price level; options handle price-conditional-on-volume; structured flexibility (proxy revenue swaps, batteries, demand-side response) handles the shape and volatility that neither can reach alone.
The proxy revenue swap is the only single instrument that addresses all three exposures at once, which is why it is the structural workhorse for financing renewable-backed revenue.
Battery revenue stacking is the primary volatility play, and stacking across markets reduces revenue volatility through diversification rather than adding to it.
For a peer-to-peer supplier, physical half-hourly matching firms most of the volume and shape risk before any financial hedge is needed, so the financial layer concentrates on residual price, capture and tail risk.
2. Market context: GB power and the peer-to-peer model
The GB wholesale market settles on a half-hourly basis across a day-ahead auction, an intraday market and, in real time, the Balancing Mechanism operated by the National Energy System Operator. Suppliers and generators that are out of position are settled at a single imbalance (cash-out) price, which is the most volatile price in the stack. Renewables now supply close to half of annual GB demand, so renewable output is a primary driver of the price itself.
UrbanChain is a Manchester-based, Ofgem-licensed peer-to-peer exchange that matches named renewable generators to corporate and domestic consumers on a half-hourly basis, using machine learning to optimise the match. By aggregating and profiling diverse generation and load it decouples participants from the gas-indexed wholesale price and drives portfolio imbalance down to a fraction of the level a conventional supplier carries. That matching engine is itself a structured-flexibility product: it firms volume and shape physically, before any derivative is layered on. The supplier-facing fixed tariff, with a commodity price cap, is in effect a cap option handed to the customer.
3. The three exposures and why they compound
Volume risk is two-sided. Renewable generation is weather-driven and non-dispatchable, while customer demand deviates from forecast; the generation side is the larger headache. Price risk carries a renewables-specific subtlety: the capture price, or quality factor, captures the fact that wind and solar earn below baseload because they generate when everything else does. As the grid greens, that capture discount widens structurally, an effect known as cannibalisation. Volatility risk is the dispersion of prices: near-zero or negative prices in high-renewable periods, and sharp spikes when the weather drops and the system tightens.
These three compound because they are negatively correlated for the book. High output coincides with weak prices (long cheap volume); low output coincides with scarcity prices (short into a spike). This is shape, or profile, risk, and it is why a flat baseload hedge backfires: firm power is sold that cannot always be produced, forcing buy-backs at peaks. 4. Options structures
Options let the supplier shape its payoff asymmetrically, paying a premium to cap downside while retaining some upside, which is exactly what a volume-uncertain book needs.
4.1 Caps, floors and collars
A bought cap (a call) protects the short side: when the fleet underproduces and the supplier tops up at the day-ahead or imbalance price, a call struck at, say, 120 pounds per MWh caps that cost. A bought floor (a put) protects revenue on generation. The workhorse is the collar: a long cap funded by a short floor, frequently structured at zero premium by choosing the floor strike so its proceeds offset the cap cost. The supplier locks price into a band, protected against spikes, in exchange for giving up the deepest downside. Figure 2 shows the resulting payoff.

Figure 2. Zero-cost collar. The effective price paid is locked between the sold floor and the bought cap; the floor premium funds the cap. Illustrative strikes.
4.2 Swing options and swaptions
The most renewable-relevant option is the swing option. The holder can draw a variable volume between an instantaneous minimum and maximum, subject to a cumulative limit, at a fixed strike, taking more on low-generation days and less on high. This hedges price and volume jointly, which a plain forward cannot, and it can be priced and replicated with a strip of forwards and call options. A swaption (an option to enter a forward or swap later) lets the supplier keep optionality over when to lock a hedge, for example waiting to see how a season's wind develops, in exchange for a premium.

5. Structured-flexibility structures
These embed flexibility in the contract or in a physical asset, absorbing the shape, volume and volatility that financial options alone leave on the table.
5.1 PPA shape, sleeving and peer-to-peer matching
The PPA shape choice is the first and biggest structural lever. A pay-as-produced PPA leaves volume and shape risk with the offtaker (cheapest per MWh, but the buyer wears the weather); a baseload-firmed PPA pushes the generator to deliver a flat block (they take the shape risk, at a higher price); a shaped PPA sits between the two. In a sleeved PPA the supplier routes a generator's output to a corporate buyer and manages the imbalance in the middle for a fee. Peer-to-peer matching generalises this: aggregating diverse generators and consumers so the portfolio's net imbalance is small is itself a volume- and shape-firming product, addressed directly in Section 7.
5.2 Proxy revenue swaps and capture-price hedges
The proxy revenue swap is the structural workhorse for renewables. Instead of fixing pounds per MWh, it fixes pounds per year for the project's proxy revenue, the revenue it should have earned given independently measured wind or irradiance multiplied by prices, settled fixed-for-floating. Because it settles on proxy rather than actual generation, it strips out price, weather and shape risk in a single instrument while leaving operational performance with the owner. Sister products include the Volume Firming Agreement and the Solar Revenue Put. To target cannibalisation specifically, a quality-factor put or achieved-revenue swap pays out when the fleet's capture ratio degrades below a threshold.
5.3 Battery storage, tolling and demand-side flexibility
A battery is the volatility play. It absorbs surplus when prices are low or negative and releases at peaks, netting down the volume-by-price anti-correlation. In GB the value comes from revenue stacking across wholesale arbitrage, the Balancing Mechanism, frequency response and the Capacity Market, and crucially this stacking reduces revenue volatility because the streams diversify. Because the asset's own income is volatile, it is then wrapped: a tolling agreement pays a fixed fee for the right to dispatch its flexibility, or a day-ahead swap exchanges volatile daily revenue for a fixed return. On the demand side, time-of-use tariffs and demand-side response shift load toward high-renewable half-hours, shrinking net imbalance, and aggregated flexibility can also earn in the balancing and capacity markets. 6. The integrated hedging book
In practice the supplier layers these instruments rather than choosing one. A base layer matches generation to demand physically and chooses PPA shapes, killing most volume and shape risk cheaply. A structural hedge (a proxy revenue swap or a shaped collar) fixes the bulk of revenue and unlocks financing. A tactical price layer adds a collar on the net open position and caps on peak top-ups. Volume optionality comes from a swing contract. Physical flexibility, a revenue-stacked battery de-risked by a toll or day-ahead swap, plus demand-side flexibility, compresses imbalance and monetises volatility. 7. Peer-to-peer-specific implications
For a peer-to-peer supplier the sequence is physical matching first, financial structuring on the residual. Because half-hourly matching already firms most of the volume and shape risk, the open position left to hedge is small, so the financial layer concentrates on three things: capture and cannibalisation risk on the generators being routed, spike protection on the thin residual still settled against cash-out, and monetising flexibility (battery, demand-side response, agile tariffs) to turn volatility into margin rather than only defending against it. That framing distinguishes a strong treatment from a generic derivatives list.

NESO Data Portal: balancing services, frequency response and Demand Flexibility Service data.

Weather and generation: Renewables.ninja and ERA5 reanalysis for wind and solar drivers.
Commercial benchmarks: Cornwall Insight and Modo Energy for BESS revenue and flexibility-market context, to sanity-check model outputs.
8.3 Suggested phasing
Exposure simulator and data pipeline (Section 3). Establishes the generation, demand and price engine every later project reuses.
Option pricer and proxy revenue swap valuation (Sections 4 and 5A). Adds the financial-hedge layer and its risk-reduction measurement.
BESS optimiser and demand-side flexibility (Sections 5B and 5C). Adds physical flexibility and the volatility-monetisation layer.
Portfolio risk engine and dashboard (Section 6). The capstone that ties every layer together with attribution and stress testing.
Peer-to-peer matching simulator (Section 7). Models the physical firming that precedes the financial book.
8.4 Rigour notes
Calibrate to real GB data and backtest out-of-sample; do not rely on in-sample fit.
Unit-test each pricer against closed-form or known-limit cases (for example, a collar collapsing to a forward as the band narrows).
Sanity-check outputs against published benchmarks (BESS revenue ranges, observed cannibalisation betas) and document every assumption.
